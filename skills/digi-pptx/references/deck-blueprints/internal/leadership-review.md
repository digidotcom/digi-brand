# Board / leadership review blueprint

**Reader:** board members or senior executives. **Their job after the deck:**
make or confirm a decision, or know precisely what they are being asked to
fund, approve, or unblock.

**The spine of a leadership review is interruption, not sequence.** This
audience buys minutes of attention, not slides. They will jump to the
appendix, ask the question that belongs on slide 14 while the presenter is
still on slide 3, and leave before the closing thank-you. Build for that: the
answer goes first, the supporting material goes in the appendix, and the deck
has to survive being read out of order.

## Archetype sequence

| # | Slide | Purpose | Layout / visual | Voice |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Title** | Topic, presenter, org, date | template title | — |
| 2 | **The ask and the answer** | What this room is being asked for, and the recommendation, in one slide | short statement + recommendation, text | direct, "we are asking you to…" |
| 3 | **Why now** | The forcing function — what changes if this waits | 2-3 bullets, text | plain, no urgency theater |
| 4 | **Where things stand** | The current state in numbers, each with a source and a comparison point | stat callouts + **illustration** (optional) | numbers, sourced |
| 5 | **The plan** | What happens if the ask is approved — the shape of execution, not a full project plan | bullets + **illustration** | concrete, "here is what changes" |
| 6 | **What it costs and what it returns** | The commercial shape of the ask — cost, timeline, expected return — placeholders, no real figures in this blueprint | comparison-table layout (33/34) or stat callouts | numbers, not adjectives |
| 7 | **Risks** | The real risks, named plainly, each with what is being done about it | risk + mitigation pairs, text | candid, "here is what could go wrong" |
| 8 | **What we need from this room** | Restate the specific decision — approve, fund, unblock, or inform — and by when | single clear ask, text | "we need a decision on X by Y" |
| 9 | **Path if approved / path if not** | What happens next in each case, so the room can weigh the actual choice | comparison-table layout (33/34), approved / not columns | neutral, both paths real |
| 10 | **Questions** | Open floor | prompt slide, text | — |
| A1 | **(Appendix) Detailed numbers** | The full model behind slide 6, sourced line by line | comparison-table layout (33/34) | reference |
| A2 | **(Appendix) Alternatives considered** | What else was evaluated and why it lost | option + reason pairs, text | factual |
| A3 | **(Appendix) Team and org impact** | Who executes, what it takes from other priorities | text | factual |
| A4 | **(Appendix) Anything else the room is likely to ask** | Pre-built answers to the 2-3 hardest anticipated questions | Q + answer pairs, text | direct |

## What to swap per review

The ask itself (slide 2), the forcing function (slide 3), the current-state
numbers (slide 4), the plan (slide 5), the cost/return shape (slide 6), the
named risks (slide 7), and the appendix depth. The interruption-first
structure and the ask-first open are constant regardless of topic.

## What to cut when time is short

1. **Cut slide 3 (why now) first** — fold the forcing function into slide 2
   as a second sentence rather than giving it a dedicated slide; a board
   that already knows the context does not need it restated.
2. **Cut slide 9 (path if approved / path if not)** next if the two paths
   are simple enough to say in one line on slide 8 instead.
3. **Never cut slide 2 (the ask and the answer) or slide 7 (risks).** These
   are the two slides most likely to be read even if nothing else is —
   cutting either turns the review into an update instead of a decision
   point.
4. **Trim the appendix, never the main line, when prep time is short.** A
   thin appendix costs a slower answer to a hard question; a thin main line
   costs the room understanding what is being asked of them at all.

## Guardrails

- **Open with the ask and the answer, never build to it.** This audience will
  not wait through three slides of context to find out what is being
  requested; the room stops listening if the ask isn't clear by slide 2.
- **The appendix is a first-class part of the deck, not leftovers.** Every
  anticipated hard question gets its own appendix slide the presenter can
  jump to live. Depth lives there; the main line stays to ten slides.
- **Bring the bad news yourself, on slide 7.** A review that surfaces no
  risks reads as either naive or evasive, and both cost more credibility
  than the risk itself would.
- **Every number needs a source and a comparison.** A figure with no
  baseline and no provenance invites a debate about the number instead of
  the decision the room is there to make.
- **Know which decision is being asked for and say so on slide 2 and again
  on slide 8** — approve, fund, unblock, or merely inform. Ambiguity here
  is the single most common reason a review ends with no decision made.
- **Ten slides on the main line.** The appendix can run as deep as needed;
  the main sequence cannot grow past ten without losing the audience that
  came to make a decision, not sit through a briefing.

## Layout notes

Same template mechanics as the other internal blueprints
(`2024-Digi-Confidential-PPT-Template.potx`, zones from [../../visuals.md](../../visuals.md)).
Slide 6 and the appendix tables favor the `full-band` zone for a wide table
over splitting numbers across two half-width columns. Appendix slides use
the same title treatment as the main line so a jump-to during Q&A doesn't
look like a different deck.

## Voice

Terse, sourced, no hedging on the recommendation — state it plainly and let
the risk slide carry the caveats. Same brand voice rules as every blueprint
(plain, professional, no dashes) — see
[../README.md](../README.md#voice).
