# Webinar / conference presentation blueprint

**Reader:** a self-selected audience of prospects, customers, and
competitors, attending live or watching the recording later. **Their job
after the deck:** understand something they did not before, and want the
follow-up. Nobody in this audience owes Digi their attention past the next
slide, and unlike a pitch meeting, nobody is watching them leave.

**The spine of a webinar deck is that you cannot read the room, and the room
can leave without you noticing.** There is no body language, no eye contact,
and one click ends someone's attendance at zero social cost. A live meeting
deck can survive a slow slide because the presenter senses the drift and
recovers; this format cannot, because the presenter never finds out. Every
design choice below follows from that one fact: earn the next two minutes,
continuously, and never assume anyone is still there.

## Archetype sequence (45-minute webinar)

| # | Slide | Purpose | Layout / visual | Voice |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Title** | Session title, the one-line promise, speaker names + title, date — no Digi company overview | template title | plain, states the payoff, not the company |
| 2 | **What you'll walk away with** | The payoff, said in the first 90 seconds: what they will be able to do or understand by the end | short line, text | "by the end of this session, you'll..." |
| 3 | **Agenda** | The chapters, in order — doubles as a scrub map for recording viewers deciding what to skip to | numbered list, text | short nouns, not full sentences |
| 4 | **The problem, plainly** | The operational pain this session addresses, before any Digi framing | short scene text + **illustration** | concrete, their language not ours |
| 5 | **Concept 1: the core idea** | The first teaching block — the idea itself, explained standing alone | bullets + **illustration** | plain, teaches, no product name yet |
| 6 | **Check-in** | First attention reset (~8-10 minutes in): a poll or posed question tied to what was just taught | poll question, text | direct question, one line |
| 7 | **Concept 2: how it works in practice** | The deeper teaching block — more concrete, closer to something they could apply Monday | bullets + **illustration** | precise, "here's how you'd actually do this" |
| 8 | **A worked example** | A generic scenario or vertical pattern that shows the concept applied — never a named account | short scene text + **illustration** | concrete, no attribution |
| 9 | **Check-in** | Second attention reset, later in the session, before fatigue sets in | poll question, text | direct question, one line |
| 10 | **Where this shows up in [Product]** | The pitch — late, brief, and framed as "if you want this built in," not a change of subject | short line + **screenshot placeholder** (gray-panel layout 25/26) | plain, no hype adjectives |
| 11 | **Recap: what you now know** | A standalone summary that works muted, at 1.5x speed, or six months from now with no other context | bullets, text | states conclusions, not "as we discussed" |
| 12 | **Q&A — seeded question** | Open with a pre-loaded first question so the segment starts on substance, not silence | one question, text | conversational, answers the seed first |
| 13 | **Your next step** | One resource or offer: the recording, a guide, a trial, a follow-up conversation — never a hard close | single clear ask, text | "here's where to go next" |
| — | **Thank You** | contact + where the recording will be posted | template closing | — |

## Two viewers, one deck

Name where each is served rather than writing for the live room and hoping
it survives the recording:
- **Live viewer** — served directly by slides 6 and 9 (the check-ins) and 12
  (seeded Q&A). This viewer is present enough to interact; use that while you
  have it.
- **Recording viewer** — served directly by slides 3 (agenda, for scrubbing)
  and 11 (recap, for a standalone takeaway). This viewer is the majority of
  total views over the life of the asset, and they get none of the live
  room's context.
- **Never write a slide that only works live.** No "as we saw a moment ago,"
  no "raise your hand if," no reference to something said off-slide. If a
  line depends on the room, put the substance on the slide instead.

## What to swap per session

The payoff line (slide 2), the agenda chapters (slide 3), the problem framing
(slide 4), both teaching blocks (slides 5 and 7), the check-in questions
(slides 6 and 9), the worked example (slide 8), the product tie-in (slide
10), the recap bullets (slide 11), the seeded question (slide 12), and the
next-step offer (slide 13). The teach-first spine, the two-viewer service
pattern, and the check-in cadence are constant.

## Session length and what to cut

Default is the full 13-slide sequence for a 45-minute webinar. A 20-minute
conference or trade-show session is a different room, not a shorter version
of the same one: the audience wanders in rather than registers, the room is
noisy, and most of them will read slide 1 from thirty feet away with no
audio before they decide whether to stay. Compress hard, in this order:
1. **Cut slide 3 (agenda)** first — a conference audience did not register
   for a syllabus, and there is no recording to scrub later. State the
   payoff on slide 2 and move.
2. **Merge both check-ins into one**, placed near the midpoint, and keep it
   simple enough to work as a show-of-hands in a noisy hall — a live poll
   tool assumes phones out and quiet enough to hear the prompt, neither of
   which holds at a trade show.
3. **Merge slides 8 and 10** — fold the worked example directly into the
   product tie-in instead of keeping them as separate beats.
4. **Merge slide 11 into slide 13** — one closing slide that states the
   takeaway and the next step together.
5. **Never cut slide 1's payoff line or slide 12's Q&A.** A poster-legible
   title is what earns someone thirty feet away walking closer, and open
   Q&A at a trade show is often the only real conversation that happens.

## Layout notes

Same template mechanics as the other blueprints
(`2024-Digi-Public-PPT-Template.potx`, zones from [../../visuals.md](../../visuals.md)):
title + short left column + one visual per slide, callout band for the
takeaway. Differences specific to this genre:
- **Slide 1 is a poster, not a title card, in the conference variant.** Large
  type, one line of payoff text, no fine print — assume it is read from
  across a hall with no audio, standing still for two seconds.
- **Check-in slides (6, 9) stay text-only** — a poll question does not need
  an illustration, and a generated graphic on a slide asking the room to
  respond competes with the thing they're supposed to be doing.
- **The product tie-in (10) uses a screenshot placeholder, not an
  illustration** — this is the one slide in the deck showing the real
  product, and a generated graphic here would read as vague marketing at
  the exact moment specificity earns the most trust.

## Voice

Teach first, pitch late and briefly. The register through slides 4-9 is a
subject-matter expert explaining something, not a vendor introducing a
product — no product name, no hype adjectives, no comparison language until
slide 10, and even there it stays plain: state what the product does, not
that it is best-in-class. Same brand voice rules as every blueprint (plain,
professional, no dashes) — see [../README.md](../README.md#voice).

## Guardrails — this repo is public

- **No customer names, logos, or identifying deployment details.** The
  worked example (slide 8) is a generic scenario or vertical pattern, filled
  in by the user with their own real, specific case — never a placeholder
  that reads as an actual account.
- **No prices, discounts, or part numbers anywhere in the deck.** A webinar
  recording is a permanent public asset; a number on slide 10 outlives any
  price change and becomes a claim someone screenshots.
- **No channel program tier names.**
- **No unreleased product names or roadmap promises — this rule matters more
  here than anywhere else in the blueprint set.** The recording is the real
  deliverable and it does not expire. A pitch meeting's forward-looking claim
  fades from memory; a webinar's forward-looking claim sits on a public URL
  and gets forwarded to someone deciding whether to buy, a year after the
  roadmap changed.
- **No win rates, and no named competitors in a disparaging frame.** A
  hostile-competitor line said in a room reaches thirty people; the same
  line in a recording reaches whoever that competitor's sales team shows it
  to next quarter.
